# Positioning, ICP & Go-to-Market

> Each entry is a reusable principle: the trigger that calls it, the concrete move, and the evidence when there is any.

## A trial form, public pricing and self-serve onboarding are the tip of the PLG iceberg, not PLG
**Principle.** Many founders believe they run PLG because they launched a trial with a signup form, public pricing and self-serve onboarding. That is what everyone builds. What makes PLG work sits under the waterline, and the entry criterion is how fast and how clearly one person, alone, reaches first value.
**Apply when.** Someone says "we're PLG" because the trial is live, or wants PLG "because that's what my last company did" (or because someone else does it).
**The move.** PLG works when (1) you know exactly which problem you solve, in a sentence the customer would repeat; (2) you know what the customer needs to succeed; (3) one person alone gets value from the product fast (with AI the bar dropped to minutes). Under the waterline: ICP and use cases defined, the shortest path to first value mapped, behaviour data (not traffic data), a trial built to lead to a result rather than show features, and a company aligned around the product as the main channel. Ask whether the product needs to spread bottom-up inside companies, or whether it is an enterprise-sales problem dressed up as self-serve. Fit is not settled by niche alone: a clinic-management system can be PLG; it depends on the ICP's awareness and the solution's perceived complexity. The usual failures: a trial that cannot show value inside its window (if the product takes weeks to prove itself, a 14-day trial does not fix it — value has to appear in the first session), a trial with no clear ICP, a free plan never designed to convert, and vanity metrics (signups and visits do not say what makes someone pay).
**Evidence.** A typical PLG funnel: 3,000 visitors → 150 sign up → 100 finish setup → 58 reach first value → 30 use the product the way that predicts payment. The three middle steps are where the trial dies or converts.
**Visual.** "The PLG iceberg": above water, what most people build (trial signup form, transparent pricing, self-serve onboarding); below, what drives product-led growth (cross-functional alignment, clear ICPs and use cases, the fastest path to value mapped, deep behavioural analytics, a purpose-built trial, organizational support) — `../assets/positioning-icp-and-gtm/plg-iceberg.jpg`
**In one line.** "Flawless PLG flow + confusing problem = bad activation. Mediocre PLG flow + clear problem = growth waiting to happen."

## Ask product, sales and marketing, separately, who the customer is
**Principle.** Almost every founder arrives asking for a redesign because the landing looks like a template, and almost always what holds the number back is something else: no clarity about who the customer is, a landing that promises one thing while onboarding delivers another, and a founder who thinks they need more features. No pixel saves that. Promise what the product does not deliver and churn is almost certain; fail to promise what matters to the buyer and the sale does not happen.
**Apply when.** Before any new screen or redesign, when conversion, activation and retention are all weak at once.
**The move.** Send the same question to product, sales and marketing, each in private, without letting them agree on an answer: "who is our customer, and what is their number-one pain?" If the answers differ, your conversion, activation and retention are measuring that mismatch, and that is the first fix.
**Evidence.** A client's revenue rose 11% after the first call, before any new screen and before opening Figma.

## The media budget tells how much the founder really believes in his funnel
**Principle.** A founder who claims PMF and a predictable LTV:CAC but keeps a tiny media budget is protecting a story, not a funnel. Founders (usually) do not double down on media because they fear finding out the product does not convert as well as they tell people.
**Apply when.** A founder claims PMF, or a predictable funnel, and the spend does not match the claim.
**The move.** Use the budget as the honesty test. An investor told a founder who had just claimed PMF and a predictable LTV:CAC: "If you trusted 100% that you hit PMF, you'd throw 100k into media right now." His media budget was under 3k a month. Ask the question of your own funnel: how much would you have the courage to put into media, and what are you afraid of finding out?
**In one line.** "The 3k a month is the price he pays to keep believing the story he tells."

## Keep the software margin and hand over the depreciating asset
**Principle.** Selling the hardware to the operator looks like giving away the valuable part of the business. It usually is not: what the end customer remembers is the experience, and the experience can stay yours, billed monthly, at software margin — while the buyer absorbs the capex, the storage, the cleaning, the maintenance and the depreciation.
**Apply when.** Deciding what to own and what to let a partner own, or looking for margin in a business whose "product" is a physical asset losing value every month.
**The move.** Separate the memorable layer from the depreciating layer and keep the first. Tesla selling Cybercab to fleet operators hands over the metal and keeps the app and the self-driving experience — which is what the passenger actually remembers — as a recurring line. iFood did the same from day zero, leaving the motorbike with the courier. **Open your own P&L and find the line that depreciates; the bet is that it is the one you call the product.**

## A product's boundary is a choice — if you cannot lower CAC, raise what the customer buys
**Principle.** The edge of a product is getting harder to define, and that is an opportunity rather than a problem. A startup does not only grow to the limit of its original TAM: it can create revenue in markets that were never in the plan, using a customer base it already paid to acquire.
**Apply when.** CAC has plateaued and cannot realistically fall, or growth is being modelled purely as more of the same customers.
**The move.** Ask the question that reopens the boundary: **if your customer already exists, what else do they need that you could solve?** Ten years ago the examples would have sounded implausible and now they are ordinary — Nubank became an ERP, Perrengue Chique launched a bank, iFood sells ads. You may not be able to lower CAC, but you can raise LTV a great deal, and the second lever is usually the one nobody is working.

## A marketplace dies full of users when it subsidizes everything but the transaction
**Principle.** In a marketplace the acquisition and operating cost is spread across a base that never generates GMV, so paying for buyers, paying for sellers and paying to manufacture liquidity produces a crowded product with no transactions. It is not unusual for a marketplace to die full of users.
**Apply when.** Both sides of a marketplace are being acquired with paid media and the majority of registered accounts never complete a single transaction.
**The move.** Stop subsidizing both sides indiscriminately and optimize the whole funnel instead, in four steps: **intention → match → trust → transaction**. Prioritize whoever carries the highest probability of generating GMV rather than whoever is cheapest to acquire. The Chinese marketplaces run exactly this play: they detect which product has started selling, buy ads for that specific product, and route the buyer straight into liquidity that already exists. The buyer is satisfied, the seller is satisfied, and the subsidy is then withdrawn gradually while the operation migrates toward progressively more profitable niches.
**In one line.** "It is not unusual for a marketplace to die full of users."

## "SaaS is dead" is a diagnosis of your company, not of the category
**Principle.** SaaS has always been dead — *if*. The declaration is almost never about the market and almost always a list of things wrong with one company, each of which is fixable and none of which is the category's fault.
**Apply when.** Someone explains weak results by the category being saturated or over, and the conversation moves away from anything actionable.
**The move.** Read the list as a checklist and mark your own: your product does not fit; your product is too expensive; your messaging is a maze; **you built a feature and called it a company**; you raised a round instead of a customer base; your costs are too high and your company too big; you sound like everyone else; you made it hard to buy. Every item is a decision someone made, and every item has an owner. SaaS is not dead unless it was already dead before.
**In one line.** "SaaS has always been dead. If…"

## Size the market with TAM → SAM → SOM (and know which one matters)
**Principle.** Your real market is far smaller than the population — TAM is a theoretical ceiling (Brazil's 213M becomes ~101M card holders for a paid app). Investors often ignore a TAM under ~R$1B, but the number that matters is the **SOM** — what you can actually capture.
**Apply when.** Sizing a market, writing a deck, or judging whether a niche is big enough.
**The move.** TAM = total addressable ceiling; **SAM** = the realistic slice your model reaches (~40% in the example below); **SOM** = the 1–5% you truly win in ~36 months — and SOM isn't a guessed %, it comes from real CAC, activation, support capacity and LTV. You can't change your TAM; you change how much of your SAM you convert and retain.
**Evidence.** A paid streaming-style app at R$12.90/month over Brazil's ~101M card holders is a TAM of ~R$1.5B/year. A repair-shop SaaS: of ~118k repair shops, ~76k do mechanical repair (the honest TAM, ~R$137M/year at the cheapest plan — already unsexy for many funds) → SAM ~47k → capturing 1–5% in 36 months is 470–2,350 shops, and at ~3% ≈ 1,414 shops ≈ R$1.6M ARR. The real pace (~10 new shops/day with a 30-minute demo each, 3,360/year, no hires) ≈ 7% of SAM in 12 months, closer to R$3.9M ARR — so the bottleneck is channels at or below the current CAC, or a higher LTV, not the product.

## Traction is a repeatable acquisition engine, not a bigger version of survival
**Principle.** The stages run MVP → survival → traction → PMF → scale. Traction is where you stop asking whether anyone stays and start proving you can bring in people who stay, **repeatably and without you**. If MRR is only climbing because you close every sale by hand and run every demo personally, that is not traction — it is survival at higher volume. What an investor buys at this stage is precisely the engine: proof that growth does not depend on you being in each conversation.
**Apply when.** Deciding whether the company has actually left survival, or choosing what to measure and where design effort should go.
**The move.** Shift the metric set from activation/retention to acquisition/retention: **CAC per channel, payback, LTV:CAC (healthy near 3:1), activation (which may dip), cohort retention by source, and channel concentration.** Expect early numbers to look worse as you open channels — the first users knew and trusted you, and each new channel is colder, so the page needs better communication and more trust while the product needs to deliver value faster. Avoid the three dangerous errors: **(1)** scaling a channel before the loop's economics close, because the leak here is subtle — not D1 churn but bad-churn channels quietly diluting an average that used to be good; **(2)** trying eight channels at once instead of saturating one before opening the next; **(3)** mistaking rising MRR for PMF when it is one video that happened to land.
**In one line.** "MRR is great, but it goes up right until the foundation cracks."

## Frame the referral prize as a gift to the friend, not a commission to the referrer
**Principle.** Member-get-member (MGM) referral programs win on framing and timing, not just on a two-sided reward. Money makes the exchange feel transactional; an in-product benefit feels like a genuine gift.
**Apply when.** Designing or fixing a referral program and defaulting to "refer a friend, get $20."
**The move.** Apply the framing effect: surface the prize on the receiver's side ("João gave you 500MB"). Ask for the referral at the peak of value (right after a concrete win, or when the user hits a limit). Avoid cash; give a reward that deepens use of your own product. Embed it as continuous in-product operation, not a one-off campaign. Caveat: referral amplifies a product people already love; it can't fix one nobody recommends for free.
**Evidence.** Dropbox grew 3900% in 15 months (100k → 4M users), peaking near 3M invites in a single month; ~1/3 of users already arrived via word-of-mouth before the program.
**In one line.** "A referral amplifies a product people already love — it doesn't fix a product nobody recommends for free."

## Pick a deliberately under-served niche as your ICP
**Principle.** A clear ICP (ideal customer profile) is not "everyone who could use my product." It's a deliberately chosen, under-served niche — and a sharp niche beats no niche, because you can't out-fight the entrenched generalist giant.
**Apply when.** Early traction; tempted to "embrace the world" out of fear of a small TAM.
**The move.** Validate four ICP filters: (1) feels the pain with real weight — pain is proportional to what's lost when unsolved (a lost lead costs a face-aesthetics clinic R$3,000 vs. R$60 for a barber); (2) big enough TAM to sustain operations; (3) money to pay your required ticket so unit economics close; (4) founder-fit, giving native language, a fast validation network, and instinct that money can't buy. With a clear ICP, failure has a diagnosis ("I got the messaging wrong"); without one, you can't tell if product, copy, channel, price, or audience failed — and every test burns runway.
**In one line.** "A generalist ERP is hard to sell; an ERP for cabinetmaking is a different conversation."

## Don't claim PLG without the four structural conditions
**Principle.** Product-led growth (PLG) is a consequence of structural conditions, not a product decision you declare. Most B2B SaaS that pitches PLG is really sales-led wearing a PLG label.
**Apply when.** Writing a pitch deck or strategy and calling the motion "self-service" / PLG.
**The move.** Require all four conditions: (1) TTV < 10 minutes — if it needs a consultant demo, API support, or paid implementation, it's not PLG (tell: full trial, zero activation); (2) ticket below ~R$1,000 — higher means a buying committee; (3) native virality or collaboration (Notion, Figma, Slack pull users in; a CRM/AI tool needs SDRs, demos, follow-up); (4) a huge addressable market with a real bottom-up TAM. If you fail these, run sales-led honestly.
**Evidence.** Brazil has ~20,000 companies with 100+ employees, and only ~a dozen B2B SaaS where PLG makes real economic sense.
**In one line.** "Founders love PLG because it seems to delete the part they don't master — selling."

## Charge your first ten users from day one
**Principle.** The first ten users define the product's entire curve, and payment is the cheapest test of real pain — curiosity is free, an open wallet demands a concrete problem.
**Apply when.** Validating a new product and tempted to give early access away to "build a base."
**The move.** Source the first ten from closed communities, personal reach, or pure guerrilla. Charge even while in prototype. When someone says they can't pay, ask directly: "What does the system need to do for you to pay right now?" Treat the payment friction as part of the test. Collect dense feedback; only start visual design after ~20 paying users. For B2C apps the method shifts (e.g., pre-sale of a solution-in-progress) but the principle holds.
**Evidence.** A repair-shop SaaS had its first 25 auto shops test the product in prototype; the team visited each and charged at the end (an owner who reached for the phone to pay was told it was not needed yet and joined an early waitlist), and started visual design only after 20 paying shops. The case is low-ticket B2B.
**In one line.** "Curiosity is free; an open wallet demands a concrete problem."

## Reverse-engineer the funnel math before celebrating an MRR target
**Principle.** Building the product is the easy part; distribution is the game. A revenue target is really a traffic-and-retention problem, and churn quietly resets the whole funnel every month.
**Apply when.** Someone asks "is it hard to hit X MRR?" or you're sizing acquisition for a target customer count.
**The move.** Work backwards: to net 2,500 customers at 5% LP conversion you need 50,000 visitors; from ads at 3% creative CTR, ~1.6M impressions (5% and 3% are top-decile — most land at 1–2% LP and under 1% CTR, so you test dozens). Then add the leaky bucket: at 20% monthly churn, average customer life is 5 months, so you replace 500 customers every month forever just to stand still (≈10,000 visitors / 333,000 impressions). The problem lives at the intersection of dev, design, and marketing — none alone owns it.
**Evidence.** 20% monthly churn → 5-month average lifetime; at low ticket many operate at 40–50% churn, so "the bucket never fills."
**In one line.** "Building the product is the easy part; distribution is the game."

## The wrong map kills more startups than bad execution
**Principle.** Startups at different stages need different things, and most founders apply advice from a stage they are not in, then blame execution. Reading about growth loops built at R$10M ARR and installing them at R$15k MRR is a map error, not an effort error.
**Apply when.** Choosing what to prioritize, or importing a tactic from a company far ahead of yours.
**The move.** Place the company on one of four stages and do that stage's job. **Survival (R$0 to R$240k ARR)** proves someone pays: validate that the problem hurts enough to pay for and that the solution justifies the price, treating everything else as distraction — technical founders build twelve features where two were needed. Design earns its keep three ways here: visual differentiation, because in a commoditized market a competitor that looks like a weekend prototype loses to something that looks like a real company; a trust layer, because a polished interface lowers perceived risk for an unknown vendor and the user reasons "if they cared about this, they cared about the rest"; and speed to value. **Traction (R$240k to R$1.2M)** answers "luck or system": do unit economics close, are channels repeatable, does retention hold. Design turns behavioral here, with onboarding that delivers value before asking effort, compressed time-to-value toward the real aha moment, and upsell pulled by loss aversion — showing what the user loses on downgrade converts better than showing what they gain on upgrade. **Product-market fit (R$1.2M to R$6M)** proves it scales: users arrive on their own, stay and refer, so you stop pushing and start being pulled, and predictability appears (put X into the funnel, get Y in revenue), which is what investors actually want to see. Design concentrates on retention efficiency, where each churn point removed has outsized effect on a larger base, plus endowment over features the user customized and healthy sunk cost in accumulated history. **Scale (R$6M+)** changes the nature of competition into a fight for attention across categories rather than inside one, the way Netflix counts Steam as a competitor, and design becomes continuous behavioral testing where 0.1% is millions.
**Evidence.** Moving monthly churn from 5% to 3% can mean up to 40% more retained revenue over twelve months on identical acquisition effort. Most SaaS measure feature adoption when they should measure value realization. If churn is not tamed at traction, each new customer only plugs the hole left by one who left.
**In one line.** "The problem was the map."

## Concentrate channels with the Bullseye framework, not scattershot testing
**Principle.** Testing ten channels at once means you never know what drove results and you blame the channel when the business stalls. Distribution needs prioritized focus — and a perfect channel still fails if the receiving structure leaks.
**Apply when.** You're spreading content and traffic across many channels with no clear read on what works.
**The move.** Use the Bullseye framework (from the book *Traction*): three rings of priority. Inner ring = at most three highest-potential channels with total focus; middle ring = up to six channels you probe with small experiments; outer ring = everything plausible long-term, no active focus now. Choose between channels with an ICE Score (Impact, Confidence, Ease, each 0–10, divide by 3, prioritize). Crucial gap the book skips: scaling distribution onto a broken reception structure (LP, onboarding, first product steps) yields no growth — distribution and retention are simultaneous, not sequential.

## Diagnose the bottleneck: no entries is distribution, leaving without paying is design
**Principle.** Design can't save a "ghost product." Design optimizes and raises the LTV of something that already has traffic; it can't manufacture demand.
**Apply when.** A builder ships an app, gets near-zero users, and hopes a redesign will rescue it.
**The move.** Split the diagnosis cleanly: if nobody enters your product, it's a distribution problem; if they enter, don't pay, and leave, it's design. Read *Traction* even if you can afford an agency or a marketing team — the lever isn't just cost-per-channel but each channel's awareness level, which drives different conversion and retention behavior depending on where and how the user arrived.
**In one line.** "If nobody enters your product, it's distribution. If they enter, don't pay, and leave, that's design."
