## Proof that links out to its original beats a designed testimonial card
**Principle.** Organic beats produced, and proof follows the same rule: a crooked, badly lit WhatsApp screenshot from a real customer is more credible than a designed testimonial card with a little avatar. A SaaS buying cold traffic has no credibility waiting for it, so the page has to import proof from outside (other brands, real consumers) or earn it through time on screen.
**Apply when.** Designing the proof section of a landing page that receives cold traffic.
**The move.** Show testimonials with the real name and a link to the original post, looking like the native screenshot of where it was said, instead of anonymous re-typed quotes. It is counter-intuitive: a landing page usually avoids external links, since the visitor may leave and run into a comment raising an objection the page does not answer. It paid off anyway. This is also why info-product sellers convert with "bad" pages: the credibility was built in the content, the audience arrives warm, and the page's design barely weighs.
**Evidence.** In an early e-commerce, swapping manufacturer photos for organic ones by hand and comparing sales, the organic photo won most of the time; decades later UGC still beats produced campaigns. On a direct-to-consumer card-deck store's site, testimonials with real name and a link to the original LinkedIn post, against the same testimonials without link or identifier: almost 22% better with the link and the LinkedIn-screenshot look.
**Visual.** A card-deck store's proof section: "Used by +10,000 professionals worldwide" annotated "social-proof bias", a logo bar, and testimonials rendered as LinkedIn posts with real names, roles, the LinkedIn icon and customers' own photos of the product — `../assets/conversion-and-landing-pages/card-deck-store-linkedin-proof.jpg`

## The unique mechanism belongs in the hero, not buried in a feature description
**Principle.** Conversion, activation and retention all start in the text that sells the access — not in the onboarding that follows the card. The rest of the funnel only amplifies what the landing page promised, so a vague promise is inherited by the trial, which fails to activate, and retention is then eaten by the gap between expectation and reality.
**Apply when.** A SaaS page pushes an outcome ("raises conversion") without explaining why it will be different this time, or the unique mechanism is somewhere on the page but not where the visitor lands.
**The move.** Find the unique mechanism — the idea from *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966): the more promises your audience has already seen, the more sophisticated your message has to be. In SaaS the mechanism almost always lives on the sales page, and almost always **lost in the middle of a feature description instead of being raised according to the ICP.** Pull it out and lead with it. Then check the chain: whatever the page promises is what the trial has to deliver, and whatever the trial fails to deliver is what shows up later as churn.
**In one line.** "Conversion, activation and retention start in the copy that sells the access, not in the onboarding after the card."

## Conversion is half clarity and half trust — answer each risk before the visitor voices it
**Principle.** Conversion splits in two: clarity (of ICP, of pain, of solution) and trust. Trust in turn is risk perception plus the number of decisions demanded. To work the risk-aversion bias you have to know every risk living in your ICP's head — and the worst place to answer them is a FAQ.
**Apply when.** The product gets traffic and converts neither to signup nor to demo, and the team's instinct is to add an FAQ or rewrite the headline.
**The move.** List the risks the way the buyer holds them, then answer each one before it is voiced, as if it were the most natural thing in the world — often visually, often through the certainty effect. Someone considering an electric car is weighing being stranded without charge, waiting in a shop for parts, fire, and nowhere to charge; the answers are a "safest car of the year" seal, a range icon reading 500 km, a card explaining the ten-year warranty. An e-commerce has fewer product-inherent risks to move, so it works the context instead — extended store warranty, a mechanic on call, 24h towing. A SaaS can go further and answer at the product level. The point is placement: the objection is resolved inside the flow, not parked at the bottom for whoever scrolls that far.

## Sequence the decisions instead of showing them — the blocking effect hides 69 SKUs
**Principle.** Too many choices produce fear, above all the fear of not choosing right. The fix is rarely fewer things and usually fewer things *at once*: decisions presented in sequence rather than in parallel, which is the blocking effect at work.
**Apply when.** A page presents every variation at the same level, or a form asks for everything before anything has been decided.
**The move.** Break the decision into a short ordered chain where each answer narrows the next. Tesla's Model 3 offers three: standard, premium, performance. Apple runs **69 active SKUs** and you never feel them, because they are split into family, size, colour and memory — iPhone 17 Pro → Pro Max → orange → 2TB. SaaS pricing does the same when the base plan comes first and the new choices only appear after it, the way Subway builds a sandwich. The founder in this case had **6 plans on the pricing page and 12 fields on the demo form**, and needed neither.
**In one line.** "Few decisions, but sequential."

## Six rules that move UX, conversion and revenue at the same time
**Principle.** Most interface debates are about taste because nobody named the rules. These six are small, checkable, and each one has a revenue consequence rather than an aesthetic one.
**Apply when.** Reviewing a page or a product surface and needing a concrete list to check against rather than an argument about preference.
**The move.** Run them in order. **(1)** Create one point of contrast in every section and on every page — one thing that wins. **(2)** A button has to look like a button, and **nothing else should look like a button** unless it is clickable. **(3)** Plans and prices follow a progressive logic that is understood at a glance, not after study. **(4)** Do something different and unexpected; people remember it. **(5)** *Seem* fast — perceived speed can be forced in every interaction, independently of the actual timing. **(6)** If you want to convert, **repeat** — buttons, paywalls, the ask. Each of these is measurable on the same screen where it is applied, which is what keeps the conversation off taste.

## Diagnose the page in ten passes, and let the interface be the ninth
**Principle.** An underperforming landing page is a system failure, not a visual one. The diagnosis runs in a fixed order that starts at the traffic which produced the click and reaches the interface only at pass nine, so "should the button be green or blue" gets answered last or not at all.
**Apply when.** A founder shows you an LP and asks what to change, or you are about to open Figma to redesign a page that "isn't converting."
**The move.** Work the ten passes in order. (1) **Context** — which source produced the click (paid Meta, Google, outbound, SEO, a founder post, referral), on which device, first contact or returning; each origin carries a different expectation, and every mismatch between ad and page is burned money. (2) **ICP** — which job the buyer needs done and what the problem costs them; "B2B companies with 10 to 500 employees" claims both an enterprise and a neighborhood garage, so it is not an ICP. (3) **Awareness level** — unaware needs the problem built and its impact agitated; problem-aware runs pain, impact, opportunity, mechanism, solution, proof, offer, FAQ; solution-aware skips ahead to mechanism, differentiation and reason to pick you. (4) **Message** — what is being sold, whether it lands in seconds, whether it matters to this ICP, and whether it continues the conversation the channel started; "all-in-one platform" and "next-generation AI" say nothing. (5) **Sales structure** — read the page as a narrative running problem, consequence, opportunity, mechanism, solution, proof, offer, risk reduction, CTA. (6) **Value proposition** — the hero needs a headline aimed at the pain or the promised result, a subheadline explaining how the product delivers it, and a trust element answering "why should I believe you"; at scale, one case from a business resembling the reader outsells ten logos they have never seen. (7) **Proof and objections** — break each objection during the narrative rather than parking it in the FAQ: price with a cost-benefit comparison, implementation with "setup in 5 minutes, see how", security with a seal, learning curve with a named three-day case. (8) **Friction and CTA** — hunt too many fields (cognitive overload), too many choices (analysis paralysis), extra steps and hidden pricing, and notice that "Book a demo" and "Start free" place completely different effort on the user. (9) **Attention and UI** — only now judge typography, color, grid, spacing and rhythm, and vary composition where it serves the narrative, because a page whose every section is image-right-text-left goes predictable. (10) **Strangeness** — personality after structure. Then tie the whole diagnosis to CAC, conversion rate, activation, trial-to-paid, revenue, LTV and churn.
**Evidence.** The page can raise churn on its own when it makes a promise the product cannot keep, which is why the diagnosis has to reach the numbers instead of stopping at the layout. The more a company spends on acquisition, the more expensive each mismatch between expectation and experience becomes.
**In one line.** "Where is this page losing sales?"

## The page continues a conversation the channel started — every mismatch is burned budget
**Principle.** Before anything else, ask where this visitor came from and what happened before the click. Paid Meta, Google, outbound, SEO, a founder post, a referral — each origin carries a different expectation, and the page has to continue that conversation rather than start a new one. The same applies to context of use: desktop or mobile, first contact or someone who already knows the brand, a dedicated session or a phone glanced at in the middle of something else.
**Apply when.** One landing page serves several channels, or a campaign scales and traffic starts arriving from sources, devices and intent levels you never had before.
**The move.** Map each channel to the expectation it creates, then check the page answers it in the first screen: if the ad promised churn reduction, the page has to develop that conversation; if the visitor searched for a specific solution, they need fast signals they landed in the right place. The more money you put into acquisition, the more expensive every mismatch between expectation and experience becomes — at scale you stop having a handful of known visitors and start paying for strangers.
**In one line.** "The LP has to continue the conversation that started before the landing."

## Read the page as a narrative in nine steps, not as a stack of sections
**Principle.** A converting page has a progression that carries the visitor from their first question to the decision. Read it as a story and check the thread holds: problem → consequence → opportunity → mechanism → solution → proof → offer → risk reduction → CTA. Order, depth, product and ICP change the shape, but the logic has to be a continuous thread all the way to conversion.
**Apply when.** The page is a pile of sections that each look fine alone, or you are about to add a section without knowing which step of the argument it serves.
**The move.** Name the step each section is doing. A section that serves no step is decoration, and a missing step is where the visitor drops. This matters most for startups that left survival and started putting real money into acquisition: a small difference in page efficiency turns into a large difference in CAC, in opportunity volume, and in revenue from the same spend. Treat the LP as part of the acquisition system, not as an isolated page.

## The hero owes three things, and the third answers "why should I believe you?"
**Principle.** In the hero, look for exactly three: a headline, a description, and an element of trust. The headline speaks to the problem or the result that matters to this ICP. The subheadline explains how the product delivers it. The trust element answers the question that fires the instant someone reads a promise — why should I believe you?
**Apply when.** Auditing or writing a hero, especially for a company with enough customers and results that the trust slot can be earned rather than decorated.
**The move.** Fill the trust slot with whatever the market actually believes: customers, numbers, logos, results, a demo, a guarantee, certifications, specialists, integrations. Then make it specific to the ICP you want to attract rather than a wall of names. **One case from a company that looks like the visitor sells far more than ten logos they have never seen** — and the bigger the company, the more inexcusable it is to fall back on the logo wall, because you have the specific evidence and are choosing not to use it.
**In one line.** "Why should I believe you?"

## Break objections inside the narrative, not in the FAQ — and scale that with the ticket
**Principle.** Anything that can make a buyer hesitate has to surface at some point *during* the page, not be parked in an FAQ at the bottom. Each objection has a matching move: price → a comparison proving the cost-benefit; implementation → "setup in 5 minutes, here's how"; security → a trust seal; learning curve → "see how this company transformed its process in 3 days"; headcount → "one person can run this, assisted by up to 50 agents".
**Apply when.** The page hides its objections at the bottom, or the ticket has grown and the old page was written for an impulse purchase.
**The move.** List every hesitation this buyer carries, then place each answer at the moment it arises in the argument. Scale the effort with the ticket: a R$100 subscription can be impulsive, while a R$1,000 or R$10,000 purchase involves more people, more questions and more perceived risk, and the page has to help that person move forward through all of it. In an era of AI-generated everything, a real WhatsApp screenshot, a video the customer recorded, or a raw result capture carries far more credibility than a perfectly written marketing card.

## Every CTA asks for a different amount of effort — pick the one the visitor can afford
**Principle.** "Book a demo" is not "Start free". "Talk to sales" is not "Try it for 14 days". Each CTA places a different quantity of effort and commitment on the user, and that choice changes conversion more than the wording of the button does.
**Apply when.** The page asks for a high-commitment action from visitors who have not yet been given a reason to spend that much, or a single CTA serves every level of intent on the page.
**The move.** Match the size of the ask to the intent you have actually built by that point in the page. Then place the primary action where intent peaks — right after the arguments that raise purchase intent — rather than at fixed intervals. The goal is to build a transition between "this looks relevant to me" and "I'll take the next step". While you are there, hunt everything that makes conversion harder: too many fields (cognitive overload), too many decisions (analysis paralysis), too many steps, a confusing offer, a price hidden for no reason, a form asking for information you do not need, and a CTA that does not say what happens next.

## If every section has the same composition, the page becomes predictable and stops being read
**Principle.** The interface's job is to direct attention: a page needs rhythm, hierarchy and focal points. When every section repeats the same composition — image right, text block left, forever — the experience turns predictable and the reader stops registering it.
**Apply when.** Reviewing the visual layer of a page whose structure, message and proof are already settled.
**The move.** Vary composition when it serves the narrative: change density, create contrast, highlight the important information (skeuomorphism helps), bring attention close to proof and CTAs, and control the pace of reading. This is the moment typography, colour, grid, spacing, components, images and animation get decided — but now every one of those decisions has a job. The order matters more than the craft: the visual is the ninth pass, not the first, because before opening Figma you need to know who is arriving, where from, what they want, what they already know, what problem they are solving, what result they expect, what objections they carry, and what action you want to produce.
**In one line.** "Notice the visual shows up almost at the end?"

## The page does not exist in a vacuum — diagnose it against seven numbers before redesigning
**Principle.** A landing page is part of a system that starts at the acquisition channel and ends in revenue. From the traction stage onward the diagnosis has to talk to the numbers: CAC, conversion rate, activation, trial-to-paid, revenue, LTV, and churn. Yes, churn — a page that makes a promise the product cannot keep sends the bill later.
**Apply when.** Someone proposes redesigning the LP, or spending more on traffic, before anyone has established where the sale is actually breaking.
**The move.** Find the break before touching the page: it can be in the traffic, the ICP, the message, the awareness level, the offer, or the proof. The more the company grows, the more expensive it gets to run that analysis wrong, because you can raise traffic, hire more salespeople, produce more creative and pour more money into acquisition while a page that keeps losing opportunity in the middle of the system keeps leaking water. Attach the diagnosis to whichever of the seven numbers it should move, and check it afterwards.
**In one line.** "An LP that keeps losing opportunity in the middle of that system will keep leaking water."

## Gaze cueing directs attention, not action, so stop citing it as a conversion result
**Principle.** The most-cited image in conversion advice, a face looking toward the CTA, rests on a study that never measured conversion at all. The original work put 106 people in front of an image and measured where the eye travels, which is gaze cueing: our attention follows another's gaze, even a drawn one, and a pointing hand directs strongly too. Directing the eye is not the same as producing the click.
**Apply when.** Someone justifies a hero image, a model's eyeline or a pointing figure by claiming it lifts conversion, or you are about to copy that pattern on that basis.
**The move.** Keep the human figure for what it actually does, which is pulling attention to a region, and stop expecting it to carry the decision. Conversion comes from continuity and contrast at the right point of the reading path, with the offer aligned to the ICP. When you do want a directional cue, test the cheap one first: in CXL's follow-up the hand-drawn arrow beat every human-figure variant on time-to-form.
**Evidence.** Original gaze-cueing study: 106 participants, eye-tracking only, no conversion measured. CXL later ran six variants of a law-firm landing page with eye tracking plus a closing question about the next step to contact the firm; the human figure worked, but a hand-drawn arrow won on time spent in the form. The figure is real and somewhat overrated.
**Visual.** The two eye-tracking heatmaps the claim is built on, from the diaper ad. With the baby facing the camera, the heat pools hard on the baby’s face and the copy stays cold; with the baby turned toward the headline, the heat spreads across the headline and into the body text. Attention moved, which is all the study measured — `../assets/conversion-and-landing-pages/eye-tracking-baby-facing-camera.jpg` (facing camera) and `../assets/conversion-and-landing-pages/eye-tracking-baby-looking-at-headline.jpg` (turned toward the text)
**In one line.** "It directs attention, but not always the user's action."

## Performance is conversion: 31% better LCP → +8% sales (Vodafone A/B)
**Principle.** You can raise conversion before opening Figma — web performance is a conversion lever, not an engineering nicety. Vodafone Italy A/B tested web-vitals optimization (LCP 31% better than control, same offer/creative/screen, 100k clicks and 34k visits/day per variant, paid traffic): +8% sales, +15% lead-to-visit, +11% cart-to-visit.
**Apply when.** Founders separate "conversion work" from performance work.
**The move.** Treat LCP/web vitals as a conversion experiment with the same rigor as copy tests — especially on paid traffic where every bounce is paid for.

## Compliments aren't validation — "what's missing for you to PIX now?" is
**Principle.** Everyone loves your idea, nobody bought. Compliments are free and worth nothing (like your freemium). The simplest validation test: try to sell. If you can't close inside your ICP, ask directly: "Falta o que pra você fazer o PIX agora?" ("What's missing for you to send the PIX instant payment right now?") The pause reveals the truth — either the real objection (your build list) or excuses (the "loved it" was just politeness). Follow up: "If it had that, would you pay now?" Then do 20 sales this way.
**Apply when.** Collecting praise but no revenue.
**The move.** Sell before building. One founder sold 20 subscriptions off a gray clickable wireframe — the QR code wasn't even real. Nobody said "paint the wireframe"; they asked for invoices and a second mechanic login. Those doubts became sales arguments and FAQ items. The gray was enough to prove the only thing that mattered: people with the pain and in a hurry to pay. Design came after, to scale what conversation already proved sells.

## "Paint the button" is judging the last line of a long cascade
**Principle.** The visual layer of a landing page is the tip of a cascade that begins at the ICP — so "make it more colorful" critiques the *end* of a process as if it were the start.
**Apply when.** Gathering or acting on LP feedback, or reaching for button color first.
**The move.** Work the cascade in order: ICP → the buyer's **awareness level** (the 5 levels of market awareness) → what you say and how → visual positioning (tone → form, color, type, space). Low awareness: open on the problem, name the pain in the first fold, then the mechanism and your fix. Higher awareness: go straight to your advantages vs. alternatives. Jump to button color and you've silently (and probably wrongly) answered who you sell to, their awareness, which pain, and what tone.
**In one line.** "They're looking at the end of a process and thinking it's the beginning."

## Scaling cold traffic is an honesty test for your page
**Principle.** Conversion measured on warm audiences (existing followers) is inflated — they forgive the page's flaws. Only cold traffic reveals how much of your rate is the page versus borrowed trust.
**Apply when.** Ad spend goes up but revenue barely moves, and the conversion rate "drops" even though you fixed nothing.
**The move.** Treat any baseline built on warm public as fiction, not a performance number. When you scale budget into cold traffic and the rate collapses, that gap is the page's real ceiling — fix the obvious page defects first, then judge media.
**Evidence.** A founder tripled ad spend; revenue rose only 11%. Warm-traffic conversion of ~3% (driven by Instagram followers) cratered to 0.5% on cold traffic; fixing the obvious lifted it to 2% — 4x the real number.
**In one line.** "Scaling budget into cold traffic works like an honesty test: it shows how much of your conversion is your page and how much is borrowed trust."

## Test hero comprehension with a stranger from your ICP
**Principle.** The cheapest usability test nobody runs: show your LP hero to one person for 5 seconds, take it away, ask "What does this product do? For whom?" If they can't answer, the hero failed — no matter how pretty it is. In those seconds the brain asks three questions, almost in order: what is this → is this for me → why should I care now. The mechanism is cognitive load: every second spent decoding what you do is a second closer to giving up — clarity beats cleverness at the top of the funnel, always.
**Apply when.** A hero trades clarity for "creativity" ("Revolutionize your workflow" doesn't say what it does; "The definitive productivity platform" doesn't say for whom).
**The move.** A hero needs: (1) a headline saying what the product does, in the customer's language, not internal jargon; (2) a subheadline nailing the ICP and the concrete result; (3) real visual proof — a screenshot of the product working, not a generic smiling illustration; (4) a specific CTA; (5) immediate social proof (a logo or a number) above the fold. Specificity sells: "Controle de comandas pra oficina mecânica" ("Work-order control for auto repair shops") beats "Software de gestão" ("Management software"). If you covered everything on the page except the hero, could a stranger from your ICP say what you sell in 5 seconds? Read with "Pass the 5-second test — lead with the problem, not the feature": that principle argues for a pain-led headline, this one for a what-it-does headline — both demand the what / for whom / why-now answers inside 5 seconds.

## Write the CTA microcopy, not the button color
**Principle.** The CTA label moves conversion far more than button color or shape — it's the last thing the user reads before deciding, so it must reduce the mental effort of simulating what happens next.
**Apply when.** A technical founder is A/B testing button colors for weeks over a 0.3% (non-significant) delta while the label still says "Sign up" with no click trigger.
**The move.** Make the CTA answer the three questions the brain asks before clicking: (1) what happens when I click, (2) how long it takes, (3) what it costs/commits. "Sign up" answers none; "Start free in 30s" answers two. Add a click trigger (the small line under the CTA) to answer the rest and break an objection: "Start Free Trial" + "14 days, no card". Rooted in outcome bias and Construal Level Theory (making the outcome concrete).
**In one line.** "The button is the last thing the user reads before deciding — treat it as such."

## Cut CAC by filtering on the landing page, not the media
**Principle.** High CAC is rarely a media problem — it's usually a filtering problem. The landing page can qualify leads before the form, killing deals that were never going to close.
**Apply when.** Clicks become leads, leads become SDR calls, calls don't close, and founders react by swapping creative or channel (or blaming the SDR).
**The move.** Pull three filtering levers so the lead self-selects without feeling filtered: (1) copy specificity — "Cash flow for service providers billing R$500k–R$3M" beats "Organize your finances"; (2) visible pricing — screens out no-budget leads so sales only meets real objections; (3) a qualification question as the first interaction (or copy/examples that play that role). CAC is cost per closed deal, not cost per lead.
**Evidence.** If you close 1 in 50 leads, dropping to 30 leads with 1 close nearly doubles media efficiency — same spend, lower CAC.
**In one line.** "The lead with no money won't magically generate money between the landing page and the sales call."

## Aim for 4.2–4.5 stars, not a perfect 5
**Principle.** A flawless rating reads as fake; consumers distrust unanimity. A profile that includes constructive criticism feels more authentic and converts better than pure praise.
**Apply when.** Building or curating reviews/ratings and social proof on a landing page or product.
**The move.** Let imperfection show. Stack the highest-converting combination: a detailed case study with quantifiable ROI, ideally a video testimonial from a brand recognizable to your ICP, plus customer logos. Avoid the common failure — social proof done badly: identical cards, first-name-only, all 5 stars, generic avatars instead of a real photo. Make each review verifiable (e.g., link to the actual LinkedIn post).
**Evidence.** Northwestern: purchase probability peaks at 4.2–4.5 stars, not 5. Testimonials lift LP conversion up to 34% (VWO); 5+ reviews → 270% more likely to be bought (Yotpo); 93% read reviews before buying (BrightLocal).
**Visual.** A social-proof wall done right: real faces, names + roles, verifiable reviews, and recognizable client logos (Volkswagen, Coca-Cola, Samsung, Itaú, Volvo, DocuSign, Hotmart) under a "+10,000 professionals" headline — `../assets/conversion-and-landing-pages/card-deck-store-proof-wall.jpg`
**In one line.** "Credibility drives conversion."

## Compress the decision window — shorten the path to action below the path to doubt
**Principle.** The purchase decision is mostly made before checkout. Conversion is architecture: the user converts when the path to the action is shorter than the path to doubt, because every extra second of deliberation raises the odds they close the tab.
**Apply when.** You're optimizing the checkout, CTA, or headline while ignoring the deliberation time earlier in the journey.
**The move.** Compress decision time two ways: pressurize the environment with real scarcity ("Only 1 room left at this price", "Booked 3 times in the last hour") — driven by loss aversion (Kahneman: losing hurts up to ~2x more than the equivalent gain), so "1 left" registers as "I'll lose this"; or remove steps entirely (Amazon's patented 1-Click ordering).
**Evidence.** Booking runs 1,000+ simultaneous experiments (per its own long-time head of experimentation); showing sold-out hotels alongside available ones *raised* bookings by sharpening perceived scarcity.
**In one line.** "The user converts when the path to the action is shorter than the path to doubt."

## Don't clone the page — the converting layer is invisible
**Principle.** A converting sales page is the visible shell of an invisible system. Copying the layout copies what's cheapest to produce; the expensive part (research) stays invisible and gets left behind.
**Apply when.** A page "looks like it works" and you're tempted to clone it for your own offer.
**The move.** Before copying, ask: what on this page is a function of the *product*, and what is a function of the *customer research* done before writing each line and placing each element? The hidden layer is customer vocabulary pulled from interviews, objections ordered to the ICP's specific fear, social proof hand-picked to resonate, and a core promise tuned to the stated desire. This error has a name: cargo cult — replicating the visible ritual hoping to summon the result, without grasping the causal mechanism.
**Evidence.** Two ~95%-identical pages, same niche/offer/order: 4% vs 0.6%. The 4% page belonged to an established info-product seller; the 0.6% was a near-pixel clone by a builder who bought that product, liked the page and copied it changing as little as possible — and who later messaged the original page's team, unaware, to complain it wasn't converting.
**In one line.** "There's a name for copying the form expecting the function. It's called cargo cult."

## Pass the 5-second test — lead with the problem, not the feature
**Principle.** A visitor scans your LP for ~5 seconds. If they can't answer what it does, who it's for, and why they should care, they leave — they won't stay to figure it out. Conversion begins on the first line.
**Apply when.** Your hero opens by describing what the product *is/does* (a feature/category) instead of the visitor's pain.
**The move.** Open with the visitor's problem, not yourself. "AI-powered project management platform" → meh; "Your team loses 6 hours a week hunting for information" → keep going. One talks about itself, the other talks about me. If the first line doesn't connect with the pain, the rest of the page goes invisible.
**In one line.** "If you who built it can't answer in 5 seconds, your visitor can't either."

## Fix contrast, layout, and trust before rewriting copy
**Principle.** The brain processes three pre-verbal variables — contrast, on-screen placement, and trust — before it reads the words, and they usually move the needle more than any headline.
**Apply when.** You're rewriting home copy for weeks chasing conversion without touching the visual/attentional layer.
**The move.** (1) Contrast is relative to surroundings, not an isolated color — a big, differently-shaped button in whitespace is *seen before it's read* (accessibility ratio 4.5:1 is a floor, not the goal). (2) Placement: Fitts's law (closer, bigger targets get clicked more) plus the F-pattern (Nielsen Norman eyetracking) — a CTA bottom-right with no visual anchor sits outside the attentional map. (3) Trust is built in four reinforcing layers: a real (non-stock) human face, a known brand or specific verifiable proof, an offer that fits the stated problem, and a solution mechanism logically coherent with the promise. When all four align, the user converts without knowing why; when one fails, they invent a rational reason to leave.
**In one line.** "Optimizing copy without working contrast, placement, and trust is masking the symptom."

## Specificity is the difference between decoration and persuasion
**Principle.** Generic LP copy gets discarded by the brain; specific messages are processed faster and generate more trust. A converting LP shows the *transformation* the product causes, not just what it does.
**Apply when.** Your hero reads "The complete platform for [generic category]", a 5-feature subtitle, "Start free", and no real social proof — the default Brazilian SaaS template.
**The move.** Fix the four standard failures: (1) "Complete platform" means nothing → "Cut new-dev onboarding time 40%"; (2) features on top → lead with the pain, feature as the solution (nobody wakes up wanting a "custom report feature"); (3) "Start free" is the weakest CTA (no value, no risk reduction, no urgency) → "See your first report in 2 minutes"; (4) "Used by Company X" proves nothing → "We cut Company X's churn from 12% to 6%". One page describes, the other sells.
**Evidence.** Across 30 Brazilian SaaS LPs: descriptive vs transformation-led ran 0.5% vs 3% conversion — same traffic, 6x more leads — driven by argument sequence and promise specificity, not visual design.

## Users scan, they don't read — give the primary action obvious contrast
**Principle.** Users scan interfaces, weighing cognitive effort against payoff, and ignore most of it. They enter with one question — "What can I do here?" — so the primary action must win on contrast within seconds.
**Apply when.** The main CTA competes with 6 other elements, or the most important action is buried inside a dropdown that requires a click to reveal.
**The move.** Make the primary action visually dominant so it's found in a glance. If the key button competes with too many elements it loses prominence; if it's hidden behind interaction it's effectively invisible. Leverage image superiority to guide attention down the page.
**Visual.** A login screen annotated with two biases — "limited choice bias" (a single dominant Google sign-in button) and "image superiority" (a vivid hero illustration pulling the eye) — `../assets/conversion-and-landing-pages/dev-tool-login-bias-annotations.jpg`

## Judge a landing page by conversion, not by beauty
**Principle.** A landing page isn't a beauty contest — it has one measurable job: qualify the user and lower CAC by turning traffic into revenue. Evaluating a static image with no context is nearly useless.
**Apply when.** The timeline turns into "AI vs human" / "who designed it better" debates that ignore conversion, while obvious conversion flaws go unaddressed.
**The move.** Treat conversion as a continuous loop, never an isolated event: form a thesis → ship the interface and collect data → find where the user hesitated, form a new hypothesis, and optimize the next cycle. The debate defaults to aesthetics because it's easier to opine on looks than to measure results — which is exactly what keeps most LPs underperforming.
**Visual.** A side-by-side "Google Stitch vs Human" LP comparison being judged on looks alone — context for the argument, not a model to copy — `../assets/conversion-and-landing-pages/google-stitch-vs-human-lp.jpg`
**In one line.** "Product design doesn't compete with art. It sells."

## Converting pages are often ugly — design for performance, not applause
**Principle.** There's an invisible war between branding (looking good in the screenshot) and performance (conversion, retention, expansion). The page that puts money in the till is often visually aggressive, text-heavy, and far from the Apple aesthetic founders dream of.
**Apply when.** You're choosing between a page that wins design compliments and one built to convert.
**The move.** Engineer the converting pattern even if the artist in you cringes: (1) one obvious attention point — eyes go where they must; (2) a high-contrast CTA with redundancy; (3) a clear promise delivering easily-perceived value; (4) guarantee, social proof, and a free element to reduce risk aversion; (5) organic images by the second fold to back the offer and build trust.
**Evidence.** The analyzed page, as reported by its owner: 100 visits → 19 signups, a 19% conversion rate.
**Visual.** An "ugly-but-converts" waterproofing LP: urgency bar, high-contrast orange CTAs, a short above-the-fold form, stat row (12,500+ / 4.9★ / 20+ yrs / 100%), and a trust-badge strip (incl. lifetime warranty) — `../assets/conversion-and-landing-pages/ugly-but-converts-waterproofing-lp.jpg`
**In one line.** "Some LPs go after compliments, others go after conversion."

## Design the features page for skimming, not reading
**Principle.** Almost nobody reads your features page — they skim three bullets and hunt for a demo video. Design for that behavior (with the caveat that the more conscious, technical slice of your ICP *will* read the detail).
**Apply when.** You're packing a features page with dense prose expecting visitors to read it top to bottom.
**The move.** Front-load three crisp bullets and make a demo video easy to find; let the deep copy serve the minority of technical, high-awareness buyers who actually read it.

## No clicks out, plus brutal CTA contrast
**Principle.** For conversion you want little distraction and lots of redundancy. Any clickable element that leads off-page is lost conversion — no matter how prestigious the source.
**Apply when.** You're tempted to link out to a glowing NY Times piece or a top influencer's video, or your CTA blends into the interface.
**The move.** Kill outbound clicks entirely. Make the CTA generate strong contrast against the rest of the interface in position, size, and color — if it isn't easy to notice and click, it won't be clicked.
**Visual.** A wireframe showing the level of contrast a CTA button needs against the surrounding interface — `../assets/conversion-and-landing-pages/cta-contrast-greyed-landing.jpg`

## Debug ICP and awareness level before touching design
**Principle.** Conversion is context engineering — who arrives, with what pain, at what stage, needing what proof. Two invisible variables must be debugged before writing a line of code: the Who (ICP) and the When (awareness level). Visual design comes last.
**Apply when.** Your LP underperforms and you reach for button-color tweaks; or you define ICP by demographics.
**The move.** (1) Reject the demographic fallacy: geography/age/income is a bad proxy — King Charles and Ozzy Osbourne are demographic twins with opposite needs. Real ICP is *buying criteria* (trigger, pain, prior attempt, proof needed); persona is just biography, the spec for your user. (2) Map the visitor's awareness level (the five levels of market awareness). Rule: headline speaks to the current stage; proof pushes them one stage forward. Selling "Solution" to the "Unaware" is proposing on a first date. Debug order: ICP wrong → awareness wrong → proof insufficient for the stage → only then touch visual design.

Awareness → angle / hero / proof:
- **Unaware** — symptom & identity / "Still doing X this way?" / simple diagnostic, checklist, benchmark
- **Problem-aware** — cost & urgency / "If you have X, you're losing Y" / numbers, before/after, calculation
- **Solution-aware** — trade-offs & selection / "3 ways to solve X — why the 3rd scales" / honest comparison, matrix
- **Product-aware** — differentiation & proof stack / "Why us, why now" / cases, demo, objections
- **Most aware** — final risk & friction / "Swap X for Y in Z days, no risk" / guarantees, onboarding, effort reduction

**Visual.** The 5 levels of market awareness as a rising ramp (Unaware → Problem → Solution → Product → Most Aware) — `../assets/conversion-and-landing-pages/five-awareness-stages-ramp.jpg`; plus the demographic-twins diagram (Charles vs Ozzy, identical on paper) — `../assets/conversion-and-landing-pages/demographic-twins-charles-ozzy.jpg`
**In one line.** "There's no 'conversion rate' in a vacuum. There's contextualized conversion."

## A real ICP is purchase criteria, not biography — write the spec, not the persona
**Principle.** An ICP is a buying criterion: trigger, pain, previous attempt, and the proof this person needs before they will move. A persona is a biography. Treat the ICP as the spec of your user — if the spec is wrong, the product built on top of it, the landing page included, fails no matter how well it is executed.
**Apply when.** Your ICP document reads like a character sketch — age band, city, income, job title — or the team can describe the customer's demographics but not what made them start looking.
**The move.** Rewrite the ICP as behaviour under pressure rather than attributes at rest. The ICP of a SaaS for auto repair shops is not "shop owners aged 40 to 50" — segmenting that way would burn money. It is **"the ex-mechanic who is technically excellent and managerially insecure"**: a description that predicts what he fears, what he already tried, and what proof will move him. Then check every line of the page against that spec instead of against the demographic.
**In one line.** "A real ICP is purchase criteria: trigger, pain, previous attempt, required proof. A persona is biography."

## Headline speaks to the stage the visitor is in; proof pushes them one stage forward
**Principle.** Awareness is not only a way to classify visitors, it is a division of labour on the page. The headline meets the visitor where they already are, and the proof is what moves them one step along the awareness ladder. Getting this backwards is one of the most common reasons a page fails to convert: selling the solution to someone who is still unaware is proposing marriage on the first date.
**Apply when.** The hero and the proof section are written for the same level of knowledge, or the page tries to close a visitor who has not yet accepted they have the problem.
**The move.** Pick the stage your traffic actually arrives at, write the headline for that stage, then choose proof that belongs to the stage above it — a simple diagnostic or benchmark for the unaware, numbers and before/after for the problem-aware, an honest comparison matrix for the solution-aware, cases and demos for the product-aware, guarantees and effort reduction for the most aware. When conversion is flat, debug in this order before touching the visual layer: **wrong ICP → wrong awareness stage → proof insufficient for the stage.** Only after those three are settled does the design get to matter.
**In one line.** "There is no conversion rate in a vacuum. There is contextualized conversion."

## The 2026 landing page checklist
**Principle.** An all-in-one checklist for a converting LP, widely shared among founders and worth endorsing in full: Hero (1 clear promise / 1 specific audience / 1 exact-action CTA / show-don't-explain visual / trust seal above the fold). Copy (headline <10 words, benefit before feature, customer language, one central transformation, scannable in 5s). Social proof (logos if you have names, metrics if you have traction, specific result testimonials, zero generic praise). Structure (Hero → Pain → Solution → Proof → CTA; max 3 feature sections; FAQ kills top 5 objections; CTA repeated 2–3x). Technical (<3s load, real mobile-first, single-scroll flow).
**Apply when.** Auditing or building any LP.
**The move.** Run the checklist top to bottom. Mantra: Clarity > creativity. Result > feature. Speed > polish. If it doesn't convert in 2026, it's not your traffic — it's your page.

## Click triggers: one line of risk-reducing microcopy next to the CTA
**Principle.** A single line of text reducing perceived risk can save conversion — in CRO these are "click triggers" (marketing calls it objection-breaking): "Lifetime guarantee," "Free shipping," "7 days free, no card." It's certainty placed on top of a known ICP doubt.
**Apply when.** Users hesitate right before the CTA.
**The move.** Find the real objections by talking to customers and reading complaint pages (ReclameAqui) and app-store comments; then test which click trigger performs best. No mystery, just work — and it works.

## Founder LP guide: platform doesn't matter, load time does; proof must outweigh the promise
**Principle.** Profits are more sensitive to conversions than to costs, and the landing page is often the first gate between a founder's product and its users. The platform matters less than speed, clarity, contrast, proof, and direct action.
**Apply when.** Building a first LP cheaply.
**The move.** Follow five rules: (1) use any platform, but deliver content fast and target an "A" on GTMetrix; (2) lead with a short pain or clear benefit, then make the promise tangible in the subhead; (3) create visual contrast with what the ICP sees daily; (4) ensure proof ≥ promise, moving from strong/shallow at the top to denser evidence below; (5) use direct CTAs tied to the outcome about every 1.5 sections.
**In one line.** "Nobody wants to know about your product's features — they want what it generates or the pain it kills. Not 6GB of space, 3,000 songs in your pocket."

## Match CTA microcopy to the funnel stage
**Principle.** Button text should change with the user's progress in the funnel — the verb matches the mental state at each stage. First interaction: "Descubra como funciona" ("See how it works"). Product page: "Quero esse produto" ("I want this product"). Checkout: "Finalizar minha compra" ("Complete my purchase"). Short (≤4 words), clear, directional. This discreet personalization raises perceived control, confidence, and conversion.
**Apply when.** One generic CTA label serves the whole journey.
**The move.** Audit every CTA against the stage it appears in; test stage-matched variants. Small adjustments, large results.

## Real humans outconvert models — banner blindness explains why
**Principle.** Highrise, the 37signals CRM, tested homepage variants with different human figures; real users and actual team developers converted best on average — above stock-photo models. (37signals later became Basecamp.) Likely explanation: banner blindness — we learn to ignore what looks like advertising and give attention to what's organic or unusual.
**Apply when.** Choosing imagery for LPs and proof sections.
**The move.** Prefer real users/team over polished models; user-generated reviews and simple real photos often beat studio shots. Chinese sites exploit this at scale with gamified UGC.
**Evidence.** The stock-model / team-developer comparison is how the test is usually retold. 37signals' own write-up of the test (Signal v. Noise, "Behind the scenes: A/B testing part 3") photographed only real Highrise customers — no stock models, no staff — and found the specific person barely mattered: the lift came from the person-photo layout beating the long-form page.
**Visual.** The Highrise homepage test side by side — the illustrated "original design" vs the "person design" fronted by a real customer's photo and quote, marked +102.5% — `../assets/conversion-and-landing-pages/highrise-person-photo-ab-test.jpg`
