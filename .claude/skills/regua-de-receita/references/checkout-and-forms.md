# Checkout & Forms (Conversion-Completion Layer)

> Each entry is a reusable principle: the trigger that calls it, the concrete move, and the evidence when there is any. The checkout is where ~70% of drops happen — it's the highest-leverage page most founders treat as an afterthought.

## Never block personal-email signups in B2B, and keep the question that defines the activation denominator
**Principle.** Two signup-form rules from B2B PLG that look contradictory and are not. Blocking Gmail and other personal addresses loses real buyers. Cutting the segmentation question "to reduce friction" loses the number that tells you whether activation is working.
**Apply when.** Designing the signup form of a B2B product, especially one sold to small and medium businesses.
**The move.** Accept personal e-mail: besides being common in small businesses, the person often wants to test with their own address before signing up with the company one, which needs a validation from IT, compliance or similar. Keep the question that defines the activation denominator: when you cut an onboarding in half, what should go is the decisions and questions that lead nowhere, while a question that defines who counts in the activation rate pays for its own friction. Some qualifying actions are deliberately kept, such as asking for the company's tax ID (CNPJ) in an ERP trial: qualifying means losing whoever is not qualified, and someone who will not type their own CNPJ to try a trial is unlikely to convert later.

## Friction kills conversion — field count, input type, and perceived risk all compound
**Principle.** More fields = lower conversion, but the *type* of input matters too: text fields usually generate less impact, while dropdowns and textareas hurt more. Phone number drops conversion; any data that raises perceived risk or invasiveness does the same. And perceived effort ≠ real effort — 5 steps with 2 fields per screen can beat 2 screens with 7 fields each, because the user is not counting steps but sensing how much effort is left.
**Apply when.** Optimizing lead forms, pre-checkout, signup.
**The move.** Count perceived effort, not steps. A screen full of text, warnings, secondary info raises cognitive load even without interaction: the user may not need to click anything, but has to read to make sure there is no catch. Ask: how much effort can I remove without destroying the business value of that data? Sometimes the fix is between things — more breathing room, less noise, clearer hierarchy, one step at a time. Your form should look like a corridor, not an interrogation room.
**Evidence.** HubSpot study across 40,000+ landing pages: 3-field forms convert above 25%; adding fields trends conversion down. Phone field drops conversion to ~13.5% vs 19% without. The rule has exceptions worth measuring: in one direct-to-consumer store, asking for WhatsApp at pre-checkout lowered conversion but still paid, because about 28% of the people who did not finish were recovered later by an abandoned-cart flow; and Unbounce's data shows conversion falling up to about 4 fields, stabilizing, then rising again — sometimes a 10-field form beat a 3-field one.
**Visual.** Line chart "Conversion rate by number of select boxes": about 16% with one select box, 14.8% with two, 14.6% with three, 14% with four, 13.9% with five.

## A coupon field the visitor cannot fill is an exit — one input cost 60% of sales
**Principle.** A coupon field advertises a discount to everyone, including the people who do not have one. They read it as proof that a better price exists, leave to look for the code, and do not come back. The field costs nothing to leave in place and can cost most of your revenue.
**Apply when.** Sales fall with no change in traffic or creative, or a promotional field was switched on for a campaign and never switched off.
**The move.** Go to the session recordings before touching anything else. A direct-to-consumer store's sales **dropped 60% in a week** with nothing changed in traffic or creative; the recordings showed a coupon field left enabled after a talk at a university. **Hiding the field restored sales.** One input, 60% less. The general rule follows: any element that implies a better deal the current visitor cannot access is a door out of the checkout — show the coupon field only to people arriving with a code, or replace it with a link that reveals the field on demand.
**In one line.** "One input field, 60% less."

## Put the input where the eye already is, and stop the copy from outshouting the button
**Principle.** On a signup or login screen the eye travels in an F pattern, so the field belongs on the left or in the middle of that path rather than parked wherever the layout left room. And whatever carries the strongest visual weight is what gets read first — if that is your explanatory copy, the action loses.
**Apply when.** Reviewing a login, signup or short-form screen where conversion is soft and the layout looks balanced.
**The move.** Place the input on the F path. Then check the weight order: if the text is pulling more attention than the login or signup control, fix it with position and colour before rewriting anything. Where an established, already-recognised control exists — the coloured Google sign-in button, for instance — using the original rather than a restyled copy buys recognition you would otherwise have to earn.

## Microcopy at the point of fraud risk beats friction everywhere else
**Principle.** Design must protect the user from the real environment the product lives in — especially in low-trust economies. One line of microcopy in the right place prevents scams that cost more than the friction of adding it.
**Apply when.** Users are getting scammed through a flow your product enables (delivery PIN sharing, social engineering).
**The move.** Name the exact risk moment first. In the delivery scam, the courier asks for the customer's phone number and uses its last four digits as the PIN that marks the order delivered, then disappears with the product; the victim thinks they are helping the delivery. Add microcopy right there: "Never share your number outside the app. The store and courier already have your contact via chat." Transparency is the antidote in low-trust verticals: in auto repair, where everyone suspects the shop is lying, showing the car owner the parts used and the technical description of the service applies the same principle. Design that adapts to its environment adapts to its distrust too.
**In one line.** "Scam costs more than friction."

## You lose money on the boring screens
**Principle.** Registration forms are where you lose users one input at a time, creating holes in your bucket and wasting CAC — while founders polish the hero. The idea is to remove cognitive load until the flow is so smooth the user doesn't notice it.
**Apply when.** Conversion leaks between signup and first value; form-heavy onboarding.
**The move.** (1) Ask less — only what's relevant at that specific step; more questions = less conversion. (2) Avoid typing when possible (swipe/tap costs less than entering data, especially mobile). (3) Explain when asking for sensitive data (documents) — increases completion. (4) Never block paste (card numbers and postal codes get copied, not memorized). (5) Help users not mistype passwords: offer an optional visibility toggle — many people feel safer seeing what they type, but a password visible by default makes users suspect something is wrong. (6) Avoid dropdowns — they hide the answers from the user; with fewer than ~6 options, use a button group instead.
**In one line.** "You lose money on the boring screens."

## Checkout is where ~70% of drops happen — every extra input is less conversion
**Principle.** At checkout, less is more. Each extra input is less conversion; ask for the minimum possible. Any new negative surprise is a reason to cancel — expensive shipping, paid shipping at all, service fee, packaging fee — embed every extra charge into the product price, never as a hidden extra.
**Apply when.** Auditing or building any checkout, signup-to-paid flow, or high-stakes form.
**The move.** (1) Minimum fields only. (2) No surprise fees — bake costs into the price. (3) Show security signals (even a lock icon with "Compra segura" — "Secure purchase" — lifts conversion). (4) Show an extra discount in the cart if you have one (anchoring works for you there). (5) Display the CNPJ (Brazilian company-registration number) — a real company converts more. (6) Show product photos even for digital products. (7) Split what you can to reduce barriers and cognitive load. (8) Use mobile-appropriate inputs (numeric keyboard for phone). (9) Yes, social proof in checkout works. (10) Test — different ICPs perceive risk differently.
**Visual.** A checkout applying the list at once: lock icon + "Site Seguro" in the header, four fields only (name, surname, e-mail, +55 phone with a numeric mask), a product thumbnail in the order summary, "Desconto 5% PIX" and free shipping on the receipt, a "Compre com confiança" trust list (100% money-back, support 7 days a week, 10,000+ sales) with a named testimonial, and the CNPJ in the footer — `../assets/checkout-and-forms/four-field-secure-checkout.jpg`
**In one line.** "Any extra charge belongs inside the price, never in a hidden fee."

## 97% of visitors buy nothing — the leaks are known and fixable
**Principle.** Cart abandonment reasons are mapped and mostly design-fixable: late extra costs (49%), forced account creation (24%), slow delivery (19%), complicated checkout (18%), distrust of the site (17%), unclear price (17%), site errors (12%), bad return policy (11%).
**Apply when.** Diagnosing why traffic isn't converting to purchase.
**The move.** Treat this as a checklist: eliminate late costs, allow guest checkout, show delivery time early, simplify checkout, add trust signals, show total price upfront, test for errors, publish return policy. Amazon shows international products with "no additional costs after" (Farfetch does much the same) — do the same. The fix is rarely a prettier screen; it is understanding user behaviour and acting on it, so map your own leaks with analytics (GA4, Crazy Egg or similar) and with a support team that feeds insights back to product.
**Evidence.** E-commerce averages: ~97% of visitors buy nothing; the abandonment reasons and percentages above come from public benchmarks (Baymard's 2022 Benchmark Report; 2024 e-commerce conversion-rate benchmarks by industry).
**In one line.** "You can frame this as winning new conversions, or as better capturing what you're losing today."

## Open the calendar on the demo form's thank-you page — the lead who doesn't book now rarely books
**Principle.** At the moment of the click the lead has their calendar open and their intent at its peak. A thank-you page that says "a specialist will contact you soon" sends them away, and most leads who do not book on the spot never book.
**Apply when.** Designing a demo or sales-call request form, or diagnosing why qualified leads turn into days of "how about Thursday?" over WhatsApp.
**The move.** Show the scheduling step right after the form, in the same flow, and remove any second form required to book. Audit it yourself: fill in your own demo form and time how long until a slot is on the calendar. If the answer is "depends on when the SDR calls", the thank-you page may be costing more than half the meetings.
**Evidence.** Chili Piper looked at almost 4 million B2B demo forms in 2024: with the calendar right after the form, 66.7% of qualified leads left with a meeting booked, against a market average of about 30% when booking is left for later. Companies that required a second form to schedule gained up to 50% in conversion by removing it. (Bias to note: the data comes from a company that sells scheduling.)
**In one line.** "At the moment of the click the lead had their calendar open and their intent at its peak; you thanked them and sent them away."

## Every extra field has a price per lead — ask less and enrich the rest from data that already exists
**Principle.** Each question added to a lead form raises the cost of every lead, with the click costing exactly the same. Asking for data you could look up is paying, in conversion, for information you could get cheaper or free, and often getting it wrong.
**Apply when.** A B2B lead form asks for company size, sector or similar firmographics in dropdowns.
**The move.** Ask for the minimum and enrich afterwards. In Brazil the enrichment is free: ask for the CNPJ, and the Receita Federal's open company registry returns legal name, size and main activity, so a "company size" and "sector" dropdown charges the lead for information the government already publishes.
**Evidence.** Marketo ran a multivariate test with three versions: 5 fields (first name, last name, e-mail, job title, company) = 13.4% conversion, US$31.24 per lead; 7 fields (+ employees and industry) = 12%, US$34.94; 9 fields (+ phone and CRM used) = 10%, US$41.90. Four more questions made each lead 34% more expensive. Their marketing lead concluded to ask less and enrich later.
**In one line.** "Why ask, drop conversion and still get wrong data, if you can buy it cheaper?"

## Don't end signup on "check your e-mail" — let a good address in and lock only the sensitive actions
**Principle.** Whoever does not confirm the e-mail at signup almost never confirms later. The confirmation wall filters the hurried more than the bad lead: someone signs up between two meetings, the e-mail lands in the Promotions tab, and the curiosity runs out before they find it.
**Apply when.** The signup flow ends on a "verify your e-mail" screen, often because the auth provider shipped with confirmation on by default and nobody changed it (the team testing its own signup knows where to look for the e-mail).
**The move.** At signup, check silently whether the address exists, without sending anything. A good address goes straight into the product, with confirmation requested inside it and only sensitive actions locked until confirmed; a doubtful address keeps the old flow. Then pull how many accounts created last month never confirmed: part of that number had already decided to try the product, and you paid to bring them.
**Evidence.** A Y Combinator legal-tech startup measured it: under 1% verified after the 2nd or 3rd reminder. After the change, the invalid-e-mail rate stayed at the same 10% as before, which was the team's fear.
**In one line.** "The confirmation e-mail filters the hurried more than the bad lead."

## Offer the payment method your buyer actually uses — in B2B the user is often not the payer
**Principle.** A checkout built only around the card loses buyers who would pay another way, and in B2B it loses them silently: the person who wants to hire is the one using the product, the company card is with the partner who is not in the room, the trial ends, the screen asks for a card, and the lead becomes "I'll check with my partner". Nobody records that as a payment problem; it becomes a cold lead in the CRM.
**Apply when.** A SaaS integrates recurring billing on card only, because it is what the gateway documentation shows first or because "Pix doesn't work for subscriptions".
**The move.** Show at least one relevant local method beside the card. In Brazil that is Pix, and the Central Bank's Pix Automático has existed since June 2025 precisely for recurring charges. In B2B, the boleto that is bad in B2C makes sense because the user generates it and passes it to HR, IT or purchasing; for the same reason, always offer an extra billing e-mail field. Diagnose it: pull the trials that reached the payment screen last month and did not finish, ask five of them why; if the answer includes "card" or "partner", the leak is in the checkout, not the product.
**Evidence.** In 2025 Pix held 42% of Brazilian e-commerce vs 41% for credit card (PCMI data analysed by EBANX). Stripe ran a controlled experiment across 50+ payment methods: showing at least one relevant method beyond card raised conversion 7.4% and revenue 12% on average; iDEAL alone lifted conversion 39% in the Netherlands, BLIK 46% in Poland.
